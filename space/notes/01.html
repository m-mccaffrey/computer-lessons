1. What Is That Light?

Unit 0 · Looking Up · 45 min · 19 slides

Before they sit down

  • One screen each, lesson open, sound off.
  • Notebooks.
  • Nothing else. Nothing outdoors until tonight.
  • Check the weather. If tonight is cloudy, set the homework for the next clear night.
1

A light in the sky0:00

  • Ask it cold. Do not answer.
  • Take three guesses, write them on paper.
  • Say: by the end you will be right every time.
2

Six lights — first go0:01

  • Let them click. Two minutes, no help.
  • Do not name anything yet.
  • Expect: planet called a star. Satellite called a plane.
  • Meteor: they will miss it. Tell them to watch one spot and wait.
  • Ask: what made you pick that?
3

You did not identify anything by looking0:05

  • Point out: every one is a dot.
  • What separated them: movement, blinking, twinkling.
  • Say: that is the whole job for the next twenty minutes.
4

Twinkle widget0:06

  • Start at zero. Both steady.
  • Ask before they slide: which will wobble more?
  • Slide to 70. Read the two percentages out loud.
  • Numbers to expect: star about 50%, planet about 3%.
  • Put it back to zero. Say: that is what a telescope in orbit sees.
5

Twinkling is our air0:09

  • Star is a point. Planet is a small disc.
  • Warm air bends light. One puff moves all of a point's light.
  • A disc is many points. Puffs disagree. They cancel.
  • Wrong idea to kill: twinkling is the star flickering. It is not. The star is steady.
  • Test they can use tonight: if it twinkles hard, it is a star.
6

Wanderer widget0:11

  • Drag slowly from day 0. Do not press Play yet.
  • Left panel is the sky. Right panel is looking down on the orbits.
  • Ask at day 100: which way is it going?
  • Ask at day 170: which way now?
  • Then Play, and watch the loop draw itself.
  • Up-and-down on the left panel is stretched. Say so if asked.
7

Backwards for eleven weeks0:14

  • 72 days, roughly.
  • Mars did not turn round. We passed it.
  • Analogy: passing a slow car. It slides backwards past your window.
  • Say: people argued about this for two thousand years. Lesson 26.
8

Which is not a plane0:16

  • Three moving lights. Let them click all three.
  • Tell: nothing in space blinks. Blinking is a rule for aircraft.
  • Second test: a plane makes a noise. A satellite is silent.
9

Shadow widget0:18

  • Ask first: can you see a satellite at midnight?
  • Most say yes. Let them.
  • Drag the time slider forward. Watch the right-hand box.
  • At 400 km it goes out at about 1h 20m after sunset.
  • Then drag the height slider up. Higher stays lit longer. Ask why.
  • Drawn to scale. The gap is small because orbits are low.
10

A satellite is a mirror0:21

  • No lights on it. Reflected sunlight only.
  • Best hour to look: just after dark, and just before dawn.
  • Homework hook: if a light goes out mid-sky, that is what it was.
11

Six lights — second go0:22

  • Exit check on part 1. Hands off.
  • Target: all six, under two minutes.
  • If they stall on one, ask what it is doing, not what it is.
12

Tonight, ten minutes0:25

  • Write the four columns in their notebook now: moves / twinkles / blinks / colour.
  • Five lights minimum.
  • Bring it to lesson 2. It is the opener.
  • Break point. Stop here if you are splitting the lesson.
13

How far away is all of it0:26

  • Ask, take guesses, write two of them down.
  • Do not correct anything yet.
14

Ordering game0:27

  • Order them, then Check. Wrong ones go red.
  • The two traps: Mars is nearer than the Sun; the space station is barely off the ground.
  • Space station 400 km. Say it as: an hour's drive, straight up.
  • Then Show the real spacing.
  • Each line to the right is ten times further. Count the gaps out loud.
  • Point at the empty stretch between Pluto and Proxima. That is the real message.
15

Light delay0:32

  • Start with the Moon. It runs at real speed. Make them sit through it.
  • 1.3 seconds. That is the delay on a call to the Moon, each way.
  • Then the Sun: 8 minutes 19 seconds.
  • Ask: if the Sun went out, when would we know?
  • Then Proxima, then Andromeda. Read the multiplier out.
  • Break point.
16

You have never seen the sky as it is0:36

  • Every light is a picture of the past.
  • Andromeda: the light left before there were people.
  • Do not oversell it. Let it sit for a few seconds.
17

Constellation depth0:38

  • Start at zero. Ask what it is. They should say saucepan or Big Dipper.
  • Ask before sliding: are those seven stars near each other?
  • Most say yes. Then slide.
  • Press Show distances. Nearest 58, furthest 123 light years.
  • Slide back to zero so they see it snap into a pattern again.
18

A constellation is a direction0:41

  • Not a group. Not neighbours. A line of sight.
  • From another star it is not a shape at all.
  • Still useful: it tells you where to point.
19

Old news, arriving late0:43

  • Read it out. Stop.
  • Remind: notebook, five lights, tonight.

If they ask

  • Why don't planets twinkle if they are further than the space station? Twinkling is about being a point, not about distance. Planets are close enough to be tiny discs.
  • Can you see satellites in the daytime? Almost never. Sky is too bright.
  • Do stars ever really change brightness? Yes, some do, slowly. That is lesson 16. Twinkling is not that.
  • How many satellites are up there? Working ones, thousands. Rising fast.
  • Is a shooting star a star? No. A grain of rock burning up about 80 km overhead.
