Unit 0 · What is this thing?

Who found a computer nobody named?

Lesson 1 ended by promising the hunt winner would be announced now. Do that first, before anything else. One point per computer nobody named, and it has to survive a challenge — input, output, and where it keeps things.

Bonus point for something arguably a computer, argued well, either way. Reward the reasoning, not the verdict.

Before they walk in: two Pi 400s still in their boxes; a monitor or TV each with a free HDMI socket; a mouse each; the microSD cards; the power supplies; the micro-HDMI cables. One spare SD card loose so they can hold it. Notebooks.

Do this yourself first, once, in private. Boot each Pi and get past the first-run setup — language, wifi, password. That wizard is fifteen minutes of nothing, and it is not what today teaches. Then shut down and unplug everything so the boxes are as they came.

What is it thinking about right now?

Ask before telling. Point at any computer in the room and ask where the thing it is working on this second is kept.

They will say “in storage” or “on the disk,” because that is the box they learned. Let that stand for a moment. You are about to split it.

You know four boxes

Straight back to their own notebooks from last time. Have them read out what they wrote for each box, in their own words.

Then put the weight on PROCESS: that is the one we said decides. Today it opens up.

Process is really two things

Something that decides. Somewhere to work.

Do not say CPU or RAM yet. Get the shape first.

A person doing homework: your head decides what to write. The desk is where the open book and the half-finished page sit while you do it. Two different jobs. You would not say your head and your desk were the same thing.

The thing that decides is the CPU

One job: read the next instruction, do it, repeat. Billions of times a second.

Name it now that they have the shape. CPU — central processing unit. It is small, it gets hot, and it does one tiny stupid step at a time, incredibly fast.

If they ask how fast: a few billion steps a second. Don't chase the number, that is lesson 5.

The desk is RAM

Fast, close to hand, and cleared every night.

RAM is where whatever the machine is working on right now is spread out. Fast to reach, and there is not much of it.

Then the hook, and do not resolve it: what happens to the things on your desk when the power goes out? Let them guess. Tell them they will find out for certain in lesson 31, and that it is worth waiting for.

Do not do the plug-pull demo today. It is lesson 31's, it needs a whole hour around it, and spending it now wastes the best moment in Unit 4.

Five boxes

Draw it live, on top of yesterday's four. Same three across the top — INPUT, CPU, OUTPUT — then RAM hanging under the CPU, and STORAGE hanging under RAM.

The vertical stack is the point, and it is worth saying out loud: as you go down, it gets bigger and slower. The CPU holds almost nothing. RAM holds today's work. Storage holds everything, forever, and is the slowest to reach.

That column comes back as the whole of lesson 32. Draw it in that order every time.

What happens without it?

 

 

Optional, and quick. Let each of them pick a box and guess what a computer without it would be like before you press the button.

The no-CPU answer is the one worth landing: a machine with input, output, memory and storage but nothing that decides is a filing cabinet with a letterbox. That is the lesson 1 hammer argument arriving again in new clothes.

Now build one

Boxes off the shelf, one each. From here to the end of Part 2 they do every step themselves and you sit on your hands.

Rule for the whole build, said once, up front: power goes in last and comes out first. Everything else can be plugged and unplugged all day.

The whole computer is the keyboard

Let them find this out rather than telling them. Ask what is missing from the box. They will look for a tower.

There isn't one. The CPU and the RAM are both under these keys, a few centimetres under their fingers. This is the same joke as lesson 1: a computer does not have to look like the thing on the desk. It can look like a keyboard.

Weigh it in their hands. Feel the vent slots along the back edge.

This card is the storage box

Everything it knows is on here.

Pass the spare card round before anyone puts one in a slot. It is the size of a fingernail and it holds every program and every file the machine has.

Ask: if I snapped this in half, what would the Pi still know? Nothing. Not how to start, not what a window is. That is what the storage box means.

Then they slide their own card into the slot on the back. It clicks. Contacts face up on a Pi 400 — check both before anyone pushes.

Which cable is which box?

Everything on this edge is a port. Anything you plug into one is a peripheral — a thing on the edge, outside the five boxes, that feeds one of them.

Hold up each cable and make them call the box before they plug it in:

  • Micro HDMI to the monitor — output. Left-hand socket, the one marked HDMI0. The right-hand one only works once the first is used.
  • Mouse into a USB port — input.
  • The keyboard is already input, and it is also the computer. Enjoy that.

The ports are all different shapes on purpose, so you cannot put the wrong thing in the wrong hole. That is lesson 3's whole subject; do not start it here.

Power goes in last

Plug it in. Watch the corner of the screen.

USB-C at the far left. There is no power button — plugging it in is switching it on, which surprises people. Say so before they start hunting for one.

Monitor on and set to the right input first, or they will decide it is broken in the first four seconds.

Watch it boot together. Raspberries in the corner, a wall of text, then a desktop. Let them read the text scrolling past — nobody understands it yet and that is fine. It is the machine finding its own five boxes and counting what it has.

If nothing appears: check the HDMI is in HDMI0, check the monitor's input source, check the SD card is fully clicked in. That is the whole troubleshooting tree today. The real procedure is lesson 14.

Point at all five

On the real machine, out loud, one at a time.

Round the table, one box each, pointing at actual objects:

  • Input — these keys, that mouse.
  • Output — that monitor, on the end of that cable.
  • Storage — the card in the slot at the back.
  • CPU and RAM — both under the keys, and neither can be pointed at. Say that plainly: you have to take the machine apart to see them, and you will, in lesson 4.

This is the assessment for the lesson. Watch who hesitates and on which box.

Ask it how big its boxes are

free -h, then df -h

A terminal, one command each, typed by them. This is the first time they have ever told a computer something in words, and it is worth a moment.

free -h — the RAM box. Read the total out loud. Around 4G on a Pi 400: that is the size of the desk.

df -h — the storage box. Look at the line for /. That is the card they were holding, and the number is far bigger than the desk.

Get them to say which is bigger and which is faster before you say it. Bigger and slower as you go down the column — the same shape they just drew.

Do not explain the terminal. They asked the machine a question and it answered. That is all today needs. Unit 5 is where it becomes a subject.

The board is what joins them

Every box, wired to the ones it has to reach.

Last word: motherboard. The green board under the keys that everything else is attached to or plugged into. Not a sixth box — the thing the five boxes live on and talk across.

They will see a real one, out of a real machine, in lesson 4. Promise it and move on. One sentence per part is the rule; do not get pulled into a lecture on part numbers.

Notebook, page 2

Draw the five boxes. Then label them with what is on your table.

Two labels per box: the box name, and the actual object in front of them. OUTPUT / the monitor. STORAGE / the little card.

CPU and RAM get the box name and the words “under the keys.” Leaving them unpointed-at on purpose is the setup for lesson 4.

This page gets used again in lessons 4, 5, 31 and 32. Worth doing by hand and worth doing properly.

Shut it down properly

Menu first. Wait for the screen to go black. Then the plug.

Menu, Shutdown, wait, then pull the power. Never the other way round.

Why, in one sentence they can hold: it is still putting things away, and pulling the plug mid-tidy is how you lose work. They get the full version in lesson 31 and the operating system's side of it in lesson 7.

Make this the ritual from today. Every single session, they shut down properly. Habits set now are free; retrofitting them is not.

Next time we open one up

Point at the two boxes they cannot point at — CPU and RAM, under their own fingers. Lesson 4 is a dead tower on the table with a screwdriver, and they will hold both.

No homework tonight. Typing practice starts in lesson 8 and never stops after that; let them have this one.
