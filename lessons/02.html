Unit 0 · What is this thing?

Who found a computer nobody named?

Lesson 1 ended by promising the hunt winner would be announced now. Do that first, before anything else. One point per computer nobody named, and it has to survive a challenge — input, output, and where it keeps things.

Bonus point for something arguably a computer, argued well, either way. Reward the reasoning, not the verdict.

Before they walk in: two Pi 400s still sealed in their boxes; a monitor or TV each with a free HDMI socket; a mouse each; the microSD cards; the power supplies; the micro-HDMI cables. Notebooks. Nothing unpacked — the unpacking is the lesson.

Do this yourself first, once, in private. Boot each Pi and get past the first-run setup — language, wifi, password. That wizard is fifteen minutes of nothing and it is not what today teaches. Then shut down, unplug everything, and put it all back in the box.

Today adds no new boxes. Four is still four. The whole lesson is taking a real machine apart into pieces and finding that every piece is something they already have a name for. Resist adding CPU, RAM or motherboard — those are lessons 4 and 31, and saying them today buys nothing.

You already know how this works

Open their own notebooks to page 1 and have them read out what they wrote for each box, in their own words. Do not re-teach it. They wrote it; it is theirs.

Then set up today in one sentence: there is a real computer in that box, in pieces, and every piece is one of these four.

Everything out on the table

Take it all out. Don't plug anything in.

Boxes opened, everything laid out in a row, one set each. Say the “don't plug anything in” part before they open anything, and mean it — the working-out is the point and it evaporates the second something is connected.

Have them name each object out loud as it comes out, in their own words. Do not supply words. A mouse, a keyboard-looking thing, a tiny card, a black cable, a plug, a screen already on the desk.

Expect a pause at the Pi 400 itself. They will look for the computer and find a keyboard. Let that sit — you resolve it in a few slides.

Don't lose this one

Smaller than a fingernail. Everything it knows is on here.

Pass the real one round before it goes anywhere near a slot. Small enough to vanish into a carpet, and it holds every program and every file the machine has.

Ask: if I snapped this in half, what would the Pi still know? Nothing. Not how to start, not what a window is. That is what the storage box means, before you even say the word.

Which box does each one go in?

One thing at a time. Say the box out loud.

Physically sort them. Push each object to a different part of the table as they call it — or write the four box names on paper and put the objects on top.

The easy ones, and let them get these without help:

  • Mouse → input
  • The keys → input
  • Monitor → output
  • The little card → storage. Ask how they know. Good answer: it is the only thing here that could be holding anything.

Leave the cables and the power supply to one side for now; they are not any of the boxes and that is worth noticing later, not now.

The box nobody can fill is PROCESS. Let them run out of objects and notice.

So where is the box that decides?

Ask it and wait. This is the moment the lesson turns on, so do not rescue them.

They will guess the monitor, or the little card, or that a piece is missing from the box. All three are reasonable. Let them argue.

If they need a nudge: everything on this table has a job we have named. One box has nothing in it. Either the box is wrong — or something here is doing two jobs.

It's under the keys

The keyboard is doing two jobs. It is the input box, and the process box is inside it, a couple of centimetres under their fingers.

Let them weigh it in their hands against a normal keyboard if you have one — this one is heavier, and the back edge has vent slots, because the thing inside gets hot.

This is lesson 1's joke again in new clothes: a computer does not have to look like the thing on the desk. Last week it was a room, a microwave and a remote. Today it is a keyboard.

Do not open up the process box today. What is inside it is lesson 4, with a dead tower and a screwdriver. Promise it and stop.

Your computer, in four boxes

Draw it live, with them, on paper. Same four boxes as last week — then write the real object into each one as they call it out.

Now the good part, and this is the idea of the whole lesson: the arrows are the cables. Last week the arrows were a drawing convention. Today every arrow is a physical cable lying on this table, and you can pick it up.

Mouse to process is a USB lead. Process to monitor is the HDMI lead. Process to storage is the shortest arrow of the lot, because the card goes straight into a slot in the side — no cable at all.

If they ask which box the cables are in: none. They are the arrows, not the boxes. That is exactly the right question and exactly the right answer.

Now work out where the cables go

Still nothing plugged in. They are going to solve it on paper and by looking, and only then touch anything.

Say the rule once, up front, and it holds for the rest of their lives: power goes in last and comes out first. Everything else can be plugged and unplugged all day.

Every plug fits one hole

Look at the shapes. Don't push anything.

This reference image has every port labelled, which gives away more than today's lesson wants — the point is judging by shape, not reading a name. Cover the bottom label row with a hand, or just talk over it, and let the real machine on the table do the actual looking.

Turn the Pi round and look along the back edge together. Every socket is a different shape and a different size, and that is not an accident — it is so you cannot put the wrong thing in the wrong hole.

Get them to hold each cable end up next to a socket and judge by eye. Nothing goes in yet. “Try it and see” is the habit you are trying to prevent: it is how people bend pins and how people conclude a working machine is broken.

Names are not the point today and you do not need them to succeed. If they ask, tell them; if they don't, let it go. Lesson 3 is nothing but ports and cables.

Say it before you plug it

Which box, which end, which hole?

One cable at a time, and for each one they say three things before it moves: which box it connects, which end goes where, and which socket. Then they plug it in. You do none of it.

  • The card, into the slot — storage. Contacts up on a Pi 400. Check together before anyone pushes. It clicks.
  • The mouse, into a USB socket — input.
  • The small video cable, into the socket nearest the card slot, and the other end into the monitor — output. On a Pi 400 the video socket nearest the power is the one that works on its own; the far one only comes alive once the first is in use.

Then stop, before power, and have them trace the whole diagram out loud by touching each cable in turn. Input, process, output, storage. It is all on the table and it is all connected.

Power last

Plug it in. Watch the screen.

Monitor on and set to the right input first, or they will decide it is broken in the first four seconds.

Then the power lead, into the last socket left over. There is no power button — plugging it in is switching it on. Say that before they start hunting for one.

Watch it boot together. A raspberry in the corner, a wall of text, then a desktop. Let them read the text going past. Nobody understands it yet and that is fine.

If nothing appears: monitor input source, then the video cable in the near socket, then the card fully clicked in. That is the whole troubleshooting tree today. The real procedure is lesson 14.

Which box did I just take away?

Eyes on the screen. No peeking at my hands.

The machine is running. Reach round the back and pull the mouse lead. The pointer stops dead. Ask which box just went.

Plug it back — it comes straight back to life, no restarting, which is worth a moment on its own.

Then the video lead. The screen goes dark, and here is the good question: has the computer stopped? No. It is still running, still thinking, and it has simply lost the box it used to tell you things. Plug it back in and there is the same desktop, unchanged.

That is the four boxes proved on real hardware in ninety seconds: you can take one away, name the hole it left, and put it back.

Never pull the card

Everything it knows is on there.

One thing on that table must not be yanked while it is running, and it is the storage box. Say it plainly and say why: it is being written to constantly, and pulling it mid-write can wreck it.

Then the thought worth leaving with them: if that card broke, this machine would not know anything. Not your files, not how to draw a window, not how to start. It is the size of a fingernail and it is the entire memory of the thing.

Do not pull the power to make a point either. That demonstration belongs to lesson 31, it needs an hour around it, and spending it today wastes the best moment in Unit 4.

Point at all four

On the real thing. Out loud. One each.

Round the table, pointing at actual objects. Input, process, output, storage — and for process they point at the keys and say “in there.”

Then the harder version, which is the real assessment: point at the arrows. Which cable is which arrow. Watch who hesitates and on what.

Notebook, page 2

Four boxes again. This time write in what's on your table.

Same four boxes as page 1, but labelled twice over: the box name, and the real object. OUTPUT / the monitor. STORAGE / the little card. PROCESS / inside the keyboard.

Then the arrows, labelled with the cable that is actually there. That is the page they will still be using in lessons 4, 5 and 31.

Their words, their drawing. A copied sentence teaches nothing.

Shut it down properly

Menu first. Wait for the screen to go black. Then the plug.

Menu, Shutdown, wait, and only then pull the power. Never the other way round.

Why, in one sentence they can hold: it is still putting things away on that card, and pulling the plug mid-tidy is how you lose work. They get the full version in lesson 31.

Make this the ritual from today. Every session, they shut down properly, themselves. Habits set now are free; retrofitting them is not.
