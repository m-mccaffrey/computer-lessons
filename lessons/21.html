Unit 3 · Everything Is Numbers

What is the smallest thing a computer can know?

Ask it cold and let them guess. Expect "a letter", "a number", "a word". All reasonable, all too big.

Before they walk in: nothing to gather. This one is unplugged except the last slide, which opens a logic simulator in a browser - have that ready on one machine. Paper and pencils are useful for the counting part but not required. Notebooks as always.

What today is: a break from driving the machine, spent looking at the world from the machine's side. It is the first lesson in the course that is about ideas rather than equipment, and it is the foundation for everything in Unit 3.

What to hold back: no binary arithmetic, no bytes, no ASCII, no XOR. Those are lessons 22, 25, and later. Today is: only two things exist, here is how you count with them, here is how you decide with them.

One wire, two answers

Flip it.

0

 

Let them click it a dozen times. It is a light switch, and that is the point - there is nothing clever here yet.

The thing to name: the wire is either carrying electricity or it is not. We could call those two states anything - on and off, high and low, yes and no. We happen to write them 1 and 0. Those are just names for the two things a wire can be.

Ask: how many different things can this one wire tell you? Two. That is the smallest piece of knowing there is.

Where is halfway?

Slide it. Watch the light decide.

0.8V

0

 

Now the real question, and the reason computers work at all. The voltage on a wire is not tidy - it drifts, it wobbles, it picks up interference. So the machine does not ask "how much?" It asks one much easier question: is it above halfway or below? Above, call it 1. Below, call it 0.

Slide it slowly through the middle and watch the light snap. There is no in-between state. There is a real in-between voltage, and the machine simply refuses to care about it.

Then press Wobble the wire, repeatedly. The number jiggles. The answer does not. That is the whole reason for using two things instead of ten: you can be sloppy and still be right.

If they ask why not ten levels - that is exactly the right question. With ten levels, a small wobble makes a 6 look like a 7 and the machine reads the wrong thing. With two, the wobble would have to be enormous before it mattered.

Every wire is on or off

That is everything a computer has to work with.

Land it plainly, then let it sit for a second, because it should feel like too little. Every photo, every song, every game they have ever played is millions of wires that are each only ever on or off.

The two questions that follow are the rest of today, and it is worth saying them out loud now so they know where this is going: if we only have two things, how do we count? And what else could those two things mean?

Break point. Clean place to stop if you are splitting this across two days.

So how do you count past one?

Ask and wait. This is a genuinely hard question and they will not get it cold - the point is to feel stuck for a moment before the next slide hands them the answer they have known since they were four.

How do you count past nine?

Write it. What happened?

Paper. Have them write 8, 9, and then the next one. They will write 10 without thinking, because they have done it ten thousand times.

Now make them notice what they just did. There is no single symbol for ten. We ran out of digits at 9, so we started a new column and put a 1 in it. The new column is worth ten of the old one.

Push once more: what is the next column worth? A hundred - ten of the tens. Each column is worth ten times the one to its right, because we have ten symbols.

Then the turn: a computer has two symbols. So it runs out immediately - after 1. It does the exact same trick, but each new column is only worth double the one to its right.

Each column is worth double

Click the switches. Watch the number.

= 0

Five switches. The small grey number above each is what that column is worth; the big number below is whether it is on. Add up the ones that are on, and that is the number.

Let them explore freely for a minute before you ask anything. Then some good prompts: turn on exactly one switch at a time and read the values out - 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, each one double the last. Turn them all on: 31. Ask why 31 and not 32 (because we start at 0, so five switches give thirty-two different patterns, 0 through 31).

Then count up together from 0, one at a time, and watch the pattern of flips. The rightmost switch changes every time. The next changes half as often. That rhythm is what counting looks like when you only have two symbols.

Can you make thirteen?

Target: 13

 

Take turns. Five targets in the list, then it loops.

The strategy worth drawing out of them, rather than telling: start with the biggest column that still fits. For 13 - does 16 fit? No, too big. Does 8 fit? Yes, and 5 is left. Does 4 fit? Yes, 1 left. Then the 1. That is 8 + 4 + 1, and it is the same method as making change with coins.

If one of them is racing ahead, ask them to predict the switch pattern out loud before touching anything.

Count to thirty-one on one hand

Thumb is 1. Then 2, 4, 8, 16.

Hands up. Each finger is a switch: down is 0, up is 1. Thumb is the 1s column, then 2, 4, 8, and 16 out to the little finger.

Count up together from 0 and go slowly - the carries are the hard part and the fun part. Getting from 3 (thumb and index up) to 4 means both come down and the middle finger goes up, which feels wrong until it clicks.

Then the party trick: ask them to show 31. All five up. Ask them to show 21, or their age, or the day of the month. On one hand, they can now count higher than they ever could before, and that is a genuinely delightful thing to be able to do.

Break point. The second clean stopping place.

What else could two things mean?

Ask and wait again. They have just spent fifteen minutes on 1 and 0 meaning numbers. Now break that: the two states do not have to mean numbers at all.

Fish for it if they are stuck - what else in the world comes in exactly two? Yes and no. On and off. Open and shut. Right and wrong.

One is true. Zero is false.

Same two things. Completely different job.

Nothing about the wire changes. The exact same on-or-off is now answering a question instead of counting.

Make it concrete with something from their life: "is the door shut?" is a question with only two answers, so one wire can hold it. "Is anyone in the room?" Same. A computer full of wires is full of answers to yes-or-no questions.

Next three slides introduce the three ways to combine those answers. Do not rush - one gate per slide, and let them find each rule themselves from the table.

NOT flips it

One in. One out. Opposite.

NOT

AOUT

The simplest gate. Whatever goes in, the opposite comes out.

The highlighted row in the table is the one matching the switch right now. Flip the switch and watch the highlight move. That is worth pointing out explicitly the first time - the table is not decoration, it is a list of every possible situation, and they are always standing in one of its rows.

A NOT gate with two rows is a complete description of everything it can ever do. That is a genuinely nice idea: the whole behaviour of a part, written out in full, in two lines.

AND is fussy

Try all four. When does it light?

AND

ABOUT

Two switches now, so four possible situations, so four rows. Have them visit all four deliberately rather than randomly - both off, one on, the other on, both on.

Ask them to state the rule before you do. The answer you want, in their words: it only lights when both are on. AND is the fussy one, the one that is hard to please.

Real example worth giving: a microwave runs only if the door is shut AND the timer is running. Either one alone is not enough. That is an AND gate, and it is why you cannot cook your hand.

OR is easy to please

All four again. Spot the difference.

OR

ABOUT

Same four situations, different rule: it lights if either one is on. Only the all-off row stays dark.

Worth heading off the usual confusion: in everyday speech "or" often means one or the other but not both - "you can have cake or ice cream". This OR is happy with both. The picky version has its own gate and they will meet it another day.

Real example: a car dings if the driver's belt is undone OR the passenger's belt is undone. Either is enough to make noise.

Predict the light

 

 

 

Six questions, mixed gates, answer out loud before clicking. Take turns - the seven-year-old goes first on odd numbers so the nine-year-old does not get every one.

It keeps a running count of how many were right, which is there to make it feel like a game and nothing else. Nobody is being graded. If one of them gets a run of wrong answers, go back to the gate slide and flip switches together rather than pushing on.

Break point. The last clean stopping place before the final stretch.

What if we flip the answer?

Watch the OUT column.

AND

ABOUT

Start on AND and make sure they can still state its rule. Then press Add a NOT and watch every answer in the OUT column turn into its opposite.

That is all NAND is: an AND gate with a NOT stuck on the end. The name is literally the two names squashed together - Not-AND. Press the button back and forth a few times so they see the column flip and flip back.

The new rule, in their words: it lights unless both are on. The fussy gate, turned inside out.

Worth mentioning if they are enjoying it: you can build every other gate out of nothing but NANDs, and some real chips are made almost entirely of them. Do not prove it - just plant it, because it is the kind of fact that makes someone want to go and find out.

OR, flipped over

Same trick. Guess before you press.

OR

ABOUT

Before pressing, ask them to predict the whole flipped column. They have seen the trick once now, so they should be able to.

NOR lights only when both are off - the one row where OR was dark. It is the hardest of the five to please.

These are the real symbols

They have been working with boxes labelled with words. Engineers draw them as shapes, and this is the alphabet they are about to meet in the simulator.

The one thing to point out, because it makes the whole set learnable: the little circle means "flip it". NAND is the AND shape with a circle. NOR is the OR shape with a circle. NOT is a plain triangle with a circle. Once they see that, there are really only three shapes to remember and one rule.

Do not drill these. They will pick them up by using them.

Two things, arranged carefully, is everything

The whole lesson in one line. Every number they made today was those two things in columns. Every decision was those two things through a gate. Nothing else was ever available.

No homework. If they want more, Gateonix is there and it does not need supervising.
