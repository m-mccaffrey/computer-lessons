Unit 1 · Driving the Machine

Where does your stuff live?

You found the storage box. Let's open it.

Before they walk in: both Pi 400s awake and sitting at the desktop - not shut down, this lesson is almost entirely hands-on-machine. A mouse each that moves smoothly on this specific desk surface; swap it now if it skips or drags, not mid-lesson. Open the file manager once yourself first and confirm where New Folder, Copy, Paste, Rename, and Delete actually sit in its right-click menu on this build - the wording below assumes fairly standard behaviour but every desktop is a little different.

Also open, mentally: a plan for where "Practice" and "School" will live (Home is fine for both). Nothing in this lesson touches a real file, so a mistake costs nothing - say that out loud near the start if either of them seems nervous about breaking something.

Callback to lesson 1 and 2: they already know the storage box holds everything the machine knows. Today they open it, deliberately, for the first time, and start deciding what goes where instead of letting it happen by accident.

Five ways to talk to it with your hand

Each one means something different. It should.

Set up the drills without teaching them yet - that happens one at a time on the next five slides. The point of naming it now: click and double-click are not the same instruction, and most of the confusion adults still have with computers traces back to never having that distinction made explicit.

One click chooses it

Select report.txt. Don't open it.

 

A single click should only ever select something - highlight it, mark it as the one you mean - and do nothing else. If they click twice quickly out of habit, the widget shows what actually happened: it opened, because two fast clicks is a different instruction, not a harder version of the same one.

Let them fail this once. Watching "opened" appear when they only meant to point at something is a better teacher than being told the rule first.

Two clicks open it

Open Homework.

 

Same idea, other direction: a single click here only selects, and the widget says so. Two clicks, close together, opens it. That closeness in time is the whole trick - ask them to try one slow click, wait a beat, then another slow click, and notice it does not open. Speed is the instruction, not the count.

The other button asks first

Right-click it. Choose Rename.

 

Most kids this age have never right-clicked anything on purpose. Show your own hand first if it helps - two fingers on a trackpad, or the right button on a real mouse.

The right-click menu is the single most useful thing in this whole lesson: it is how you rename, copy, and delete without dragging anything anywhere. Everything in Part 2 uses this menu. If they only remember one thing from the drills, make it this one.

Pick it up. Put it down.

Drag the file into the folder.

Pictures

 

Press and hold, move, let go over the target. This is the physically hardest of the five for a seven-year-old - small hands and a mouse that is not sized for them make the hold-while-moving part genuinely tricky. Let them retry with Reset as many times as it takes without comment.

Say what dragging actually does before they try it on the real machine in Part 2: moves the thing, does not copy it. That surprises people who assume drag is safe because it feels gentle.

There's always more below

Scroll down. Click homework.txt.

 

A folder with more in it than fits on screen is the normal case, not the exception, and a surprising number of adults never fully realise that what they can see is not always everything that is there.

If they click a wrong item, the widget says so gently and they keep going - there is no penalty for guessing, only for not looking.

That's the whole mouse

Five moves. Everything else is combinations.

Natural break point. If today is running long or attention is going, stop here and pick up Part 2 another day - nothing below depends on finishing in one sitting.

If you're continuing: stand up, stretch, then sit back down at the real machine. Part 2 is entirely on the actual Pi, not the slides.

Find the file manager

One click. It's on the taskbar.

Point at it once if they can't find it, then never again - they will need to find this icon unprompted for the rest of the course. On stock Raspberry Pi OS it looks like a filing cabinet or a drawer.

It should open showing the Home folder. If it opens somewhere else, click Home in the sidebar before continuing.

Where are you right now?

Read it before I tell you.

Point at the address bar or breadcrumb without saying what it means, and ask them to read it out loud. "Home" is the answer at the start - the top of their own personal storage, same idea as the desk in lesson 2's storage box, just for files instead of the whole machine.

Go two folders deep

Then find your way back.

Open any existing folder (Desktop, Documents, Pictures all usually exist already), then one of its subfolders if it has one - if not, that's fine, one level deep still makes the point. Watch the breadcrumb grow a step each time.

Then back to Home using the Home button or the breadcrumb, not the back arrow - the back arrow retraces clicks, the breadcrumb jumps straight there, and the difference between those two is worth them noticing.

Make one

Right-click empty space. New Folder.

Empty space inside the Home window, not on an existing icon. The option might read "New Folder" directly or live under a "Create New" submenu - look before the lesson so you're not hunting live.

Name it Practice. Everything for the rest of Part 2 happens to this one folder, so nothing that matters is at risk.

Copy leaves two

Copy Practice. Paste it. Count the folders.

Right-click Practice → Copy, then right-click empty space → Paste. A second folder appears, probably named "Practice (copy)" or similar. Two folders now exist where there was one a moment ago - say that plainly, it is the whole definition of copy.

Move leaves one

Drag the copy into the original.

Drag "Practice (copy)" onto the Practice folder icon and let go. It disappears from the top level - not deleted, just relocated, now living inside Practice. Open Practice together and find it in there to prove it.

While it's selected, right-click → Rename it to Trash Me - this reuses the rename skill from the drills, and sets up the next slide cleanly.

Contrast worth saying out loud: copy made two. Move never changes the count, it only changes where the one thing is.

Delete it, safely

Right-click Trash Me. Delete.

It should vanish from view and land in the Trash or Wastebasket, not disappear forever. Open the Trash together and find it sitting there, still recoverable.

Say clearly: deleting is not permanent until the trash itself is emptied, which is a separate, deliberate action they are not doing today. That is the whole reason deleting something you're not sure about is safe to try.

A good name explains itself

Not "New Folder (2)."

Quick, not a lecture. A name should say what's inside without opening it - "Math Homework" beats "stuff" and beats an unrenamed "New Folder (2)" left over from not finishing the job. Ask them what's wrong with naming everything "New Folder" - they will get it immediately once asked.

Build School

One folder per real subject. Then go deeper.

The main event. In Home, make a folder called School. Inside it, one folder per actual subject they take - Math, Science, Reading, whatever their real classes are, not a list you hand them. Inside each subject, 2-3 folders for the kinds of thing that pile up: Homework, Notes, Tests is a good default if they're stuck for ideas.

That's three levels under Home: School → Math → Homework. Every skill from today is in this one task - New Folder, Rename if they mistype, and organizing on purpose instead of by accident.

Let the two of them build genuinely different structures if their real subjects differ. This is not a test with one right answer; it is their filing system, for their actual schoolwork, starting today.

Go one level deeper

If there's time. This month's homework gets its own folder.

Optional stretch, mainly for the older one if the younger one is done and happy. Inside Math → Homework, a folder for the current month reaches a fourth level: School → Math → Homework → October. Not required - three levels is a complete answer to today's goal.

Walk your own tree

Get to one folder. No help, no hints.

Name a specific destination inside their own new structure - "get to where Science homework goes" - and watch them navigate there with no help. This is the real assessment for the whole lesson: can they find their way through something they built, using only what they learned today.

If they get lost, that's useful information, not a failure - ask what they expected to see at each click, and it usually reveals exactly which folder needs a better name.

Not losing your files starts with a folder that makes sense

That line is the course's own thesis, almost word for word - this lesson is where it stops being an idea and starts being something they actually did.

Homework, genuinely: keep using School for real classwork as it comes in. Not a worksheet, just the habit continuing past today.
